Fast and Deep

Fast combines discovery, clip construction and scoring into one model round, followed by deterministic ranking. Deep first engineers candidate regions from transcript boundaries and inexpensive audio-visual signals, merges and prunes them in software, then makes one multimodal judgement per shortlisted region.

Deep replaced a chain of up to five sequential model stages. Its core dependency depth is one round — two with optional criteria compilation. That is sequential depth, not total calls: several shortlisted regions can each need a call.

Spend analysis where the source needs it

Speech density and cut rate decide whether face sampling, motion analysis or on-screen graphics are worth running; an interview and a screen recording should not pay for identical analysis. Expensive stages store durable outputs keyed by stable input hashes, so retries reuse matching work, and a change of prompt, model or configuration invalidates it through the input identity.

Reframing: intent from the model, geometry from code

Basic framing uses classical computer vision and produces the default editable composition. Advanced RAVA combines object detection and tracking with faces, speaker evidence, graphics and activity, then shows a multimodal Director labelled planning imagery and structured evidence. The Director chooses who or what to frame and how; deterministic code resolves targets and computes crops, layouts and camera paths. The output is editable geometry, encoded later.

RAVA runs as a durable job and produces a candidate tied to the editor revision it observed. Worker completion alone never changes the accepted composition; acceptance goes through the authoring gateway.

Inside RAVA

RAVA is the largest engine in the codebase. Its perception layer combines face detection, face tracks and face-to-subject association, a LightASD active-speaker engine, pose, on-screen graphics and activity analysis into labelled evidence. A planner and the multimodal Director turn that into intent; an intent resolver maps the Director’s choices onto real tracked subjects; feasibility checks decide whether a framing is physically achievable in 9:16 before geometry is computed; and dedicated modules handle group crops, multi-subject layouts, camera motion and fallbacks when evidence is weak.

Clyflo also keeps a deliberately non-intelligent default renderer: one geometry rule, scale-to-fit on a fixed canvas, applied the same way to every clip. Subject-aware framing belongs to RAVA alone, and the default renderer is not allowed to grow toward it — two clear responsibilities instead of one blurry one.

Measuring before redesigning

I built an evaluation harness so “selection quality isn’t good” became measurable before any redesign. It scores selection recall against human-labelled golden moments, broken down by modality — dialogue, visual, audio, reaction — and by content type such as podcast, gaming, livestream or interview, plus clip-length compliance. The modality split is the point: a transcript-driven selector should do well on dialogue and poorly on visual or reaction moments, and that measured gap is what the multimodal redesign had to close.

Measuring the result

We measured retention, framing coverage and camera movement, and kept execution profiles and artifacts for diagnosis. The release shipped with known quality limits and before its intended broad benchmark, so I describe it as a deployed AI-assisted reframing workflow, not proven editorial superiority. A comparative self-review mode exists in the code but is off by default.

Implementation notes

Latency versus cost

Reducing sequential depth shortens the critical path; it does not by itself reduce total provider calls. Both are tracked because both matter.
